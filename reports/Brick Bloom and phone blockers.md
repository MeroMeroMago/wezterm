# Lock the feeds, keep the wallet

The best minimalist setup is not a dumbphone. It is a phone that keeps the six essentials (tap-to-pay, banking, 2FA, maps, rideshare, messaging) and puts everything else behind a lock you can't casually undo. On an iPhone, the lock with the best mix of strictness, price and track record is **Brick: $59 once, no subscription, run in Strict Mode with an allow-list of essential apps**, backed by a Screen Time passcode that someone else holds. Its free equivalent is the open-source **Foqos app plus a roughly $9 pack of NFC stickers**. **Bloom ($39)** is the better buy only if you want built-in five-minute breaks, a parental Child Mode or a wallet-sized card, because it is deliberately softer than Brick. On Android, every blocker (token or app) runs on an Accessibility Service that the phone's owner can switch off. That includes the Unihertz Titan 2 Elite ($489.99, shipping now) and the Clicks Communicator ($499 through September 30, then $649, shipping December 2026). On those phones the strongest "blocker" is simply **never installing the feed apps**. A token or a strict-mode app such as AppBlock or Lock Me Out then acts only as a speed bump for the browser and one or two borderline apps. Nothing beats a timed lockbox (kSafe $59, Mindsight from $39.95), but a lockbox also locks away your wallet, maps and 2FA, so it belongs on the phone you leave at home. No independent study has tested Brick, Bloom or any other token. The peer-reviewed evidence supports categories of intervention instead: blocking mobile internet, friction prompts, self-set limits and grayscale. A final caveat applies to everything below. The researchers' page fetches were blocked, so every fact comes from search-engine snippets of the cited pages. Many of the "Brick vs X" comparisons were written by competing blocker vendors or affiliate blogs.

## Every blocker is only as strong as its escape hatch in Settings

The single most useful fact for choosing a blocker is that **on iPhone, every third-party blocker sits on the same Apple framework**. Brick, Bloom, Opal, one sec, ScreenZen, Jomo, Foqos and the rest all use the Screen Time API (FamilyControls plus ManagedSettings) to put a "shield" over chosen apps ([boring-phone README](https://github.com/MellevdB/boring-phone); [Cybernews](https://cybernews.com/reviews/brick-phone-blocker-review/)). Shields are enforced by iOS itself, so force-quitting the blocker does nothing. That leaves three main escape routes. The first is deleting the blocker app, which "by default" lifts the block for Bloom ([Bloom FAQ](https://bloom.inc/pages/faq)) and historically did for Brick ([whatifididnt](https://whatifididnt.com/blog/brick-phone-app/)). The second is switching off the app's entry under Settings > Screen Time > Apps with Screen Time Access, a hole that "plagues virtually all third-party distraction management apps on iOS" ([Password Locker](https://password-locker.com/blog/post/opal-workarounds-a-solution-to-disabling-screen-time-access/), a competitor). The third is a set of web-shaped leaks: in-app browsers, links in notes apps and the Passwords app's browser ([Apple Developer Forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/127748); [whatifididnt](https://whatifididnt.com/blog/brick-phone-app/)).

Vendors close the first route with "Strict Mode" (an anti-delete lock). The second route got a real fix in **iOS 26.4**. There is now an optional setting that makes revoking a blocker's Screen Time access require the **Screen Time passcode** instead of Face ID. It is off by default ([Tech Lockdown](https://www.techlockdown.com/articles/ios26-update-screen-time-protected-app-permissions)), and a developer-forum thread reports a bug where iOS 26.4 still asks for Face ID ([Apple Developer Forums 821959](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/821959)). The passcode has its own weakness. Anyone who knows the Apple Account email and password used to set it can reset it ([Apple Support](https://support.apple.com/en-mide/102677)), so a partner-held passcode only holds if it is tied to an account you can't log into. The platform is also shakier than usual. One sec's developer has documented iOS 26 Screen Time API regressions that were still open ten months after being reported: usage thresholds that don't fire, shields that don't update, and corrupted usage data ([riedel.wtf](https://riedel.wtf/state-of-the-screen-time-api-2024/); [Apple Developer Forums 819997](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/819997)). Those bugs hit every iOS blocker, hardware or not.

**Android works differently, and worse, for self-binding.** "Android has no API that prevents an app opening, so blockers draw a screen over the app after it launches, making an Android block a cover rather than a lock" ([Habit Doom](https://habitdoom.com/blog/best-app-blockers-android-2026), a competitor, but technically accurate). The cover runs through an Accessibility Service, and the owner can turn it off in Settings. Blockers add Device Admin rights to resist uninstalling. Even so, force-stop, clearing app data, booting into safe mode (which disables all non-preinstalled apps), ADB and a factory reset all remain available ([Habit Doom](https://habitdoom.com/blog/android-app-blocker-that-cant-be-bypassed); [XDA](https://xdaforums.com/t/blocking-safe-mode-and-factory-reset-on-android.4603707/)). One open-source developer admits their strict mode is "a best-effort deterrent… A sufficiently motivated user can always reach a recovery path" ([Foqos-Android](https://github.com/Elias02345/Foqos-Android)). There is a new wrinkle in 2026. **Android 17's Advanced Protection Mode revokes Accessibility access from any app not flagged as an accessibility tool**, and the only override is turning the mode off ([Android Authority](https://www.androidauthority.com/android-17-beta-2-advanced-protection-mode-accessibility-apps-3648860/); [The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2026/03/android-17-blocks-non-accessibility.html)). AppBlock has already published a notice that it is affected ([AppBlock](https://appblock.app/androids-new-advanced-protection-affects-appblock-from-blocking/)). The Clicks Communicator launches on Android 17 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clicks_Communicator)), so its owners will have to choose between that security hardening and a working blocker.

The table below ranks the categories by how hard they are to escape. It is the researchers' inference from each product's stated design; no source has published a systematic, independent test of how easily each blocker can be bypassed.

| Rank | Category | Examples | iPhone escape route | Android escape route |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Timed lockbox | kSafe, Mindsight Fortress Mode | None short of breaking the box | Same |
| 2 | Lock-by-default token | Autonomous Key | Revoke Screen Time access, unless the passcode guards it | Accessibility off, safe mode, ADB |
| 3 | NFC or proximity token with anti-delete | Brick Strict, Bloom Strict, Blok, Foqos, Halo | Revoke Screen Time access, unless the passcode guards it; emergency unlocks | Accessibility off, mini-window tricks, safe mode |
| 4 | Paid strict-mode apps | Opal Deep Focus, Jomo Hard, AppBlock Strict, Lock Me Out | Same as rank 3 | Same as rank 3 |
| 5 | Friction apps and launchers | one sec, ScreenZen, Blank Spaces, Olauncher | Tap through the prompt | Tap through; change launcher |
| 6 | Built-in nudges | Screen Time without passcode, Digital Wellbeing | "Ignore limit" | 5-minute override |

Two conclusions follow. On iPhone, paying more for a token buys **user experience and a physical ritual, not a stronger lock**, because the lock is Apple's in every case. The biggest strength upgrade is free: the iOS 26.4 protection plus a passcode you don't control. On Android the ceiling is lower, so the most reliable restriction is one that doesn't depend on software: not installing the app, or keeping it on a different device.

## Brick and Bloom split the market between strict and forgiving

### Brick is a $59 puck that stays locked until you walk back to it

Brick is a battery-free, magnet-backed NFC puck of roughly 1.5–2 inches in 3D-printed plastic. It sells for **$59 one-time, or two for $106.20**, in grey, charcoal or white. That price covers the hardware and lifetime use of the free app, with "no subscriptions, no extra fees" ([Cybernews](https://cybernews.com/reviews/brick-phone-blocker-review/); [Brick FAQ](https://getbrick.com/pages/faq); [Writer Gadgets](https://writergadgets.com/brick-app-review/)). It launched at $49 in September 2023, when the founders were 3D-printing units in a basement. The founders are UW-Madison graduates TJ Driver and Zach Nasgowitz of Germantown, Wisconsin, and the company is bootstrapped ([BizTimes](https://biztimes.com/jump-start-germantown-startup-brick-aims-to-temporarily-block-distractions-on-smartphones/)). Sales run periodically, including a back-to-school discount covered by ABC News in 2026 ([ABC News/GMA](https://abcnews.com/GMA/Shop/brick-review/story?id=134547488)). It is also sold on Amazon ([Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/Brick-Phone-Blocker-Device-Lock-Screen/dp/B0GQ6VV79M)). One 2026 snippet claims Brick is now HSA/FSA eligible, but the researchers could not tie that claim to a specific page. No "Brick 2" or new hardware turned up as of late September 2026.

To use it, you pick apps in the app and tap the phone to the puck. The chosen apps are shielded and their notifications silenced. They stay that way until you tap the same puck again ([Cybernews](https://cybernews.com/reviews/brick-phone-blocker-review/)). You can start a session without the puck by holding the virtual Brick on screen for five seconds, but **ending one requires the physical puck** or an emergency unbrick ([Brick FAQ](https://getbrick.com/pages/faq)). The app supports named Modes such as Work, Sleep and Gym, with up to 50 apps each ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/brick-ditch-distractions/id6448794069)). A mode can also be flipped into an allow-list, where "you choose only the apps you want to keep — everything else gets locked" ([whatifididnt](https://whatifididnt.com/blog/brick-phone-app/)). That allow-list is the feature that turns an iPhone into an essentials-only phone. Scheduled auto-Bricking exists, though one comparison says schedules set only a start time and you still tap to end ([whatifididnt: Unpluq vs Brick](https://whatifididnt.com/blog/unpluq-vs-brick/)); that detail may be out of date. Brick can block Safari entirely or specific domains in Safari. It "cannot block specific website links in non-Safari browsers," so block Chrome and other browsers wholesale ([Brick help](https://getbrick.com/pages/need-help-blocking)). Apple doesn't allow the phone dialer to be blocked, so calls always work ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/brick-ditch-distractions/id6448794069)).

**Strict Mode** "makes it impossible to delete the Brick app from your phone or disable the app through your phone's screen time settings" ([Brick help center](https://brick.frontkb.com/en/articles/6572993)). Without it, Brick is only friction. The escape valve is **five Emergency Unbricks**, which don't refresh automatically. Sources disagree on whether the five are per device or a lifetime total per account. Refills come by form or email to support, typically in about 48 hours or two business days ([Cybernews](https://cybernews.com/reviews/brick-phone-blocker-review/); [whatifididnt](https://whatifididnt.com/blog/brick-phone-app/); [NBC Select](https://www.nbcnews.com/select/shopping/brick-phone-app-blocker-review-rcna259740)). If you lose the puck, the emergency unbricks are the only way out. "If you run out… the only way back into blocked apps is by scanning the actual device" ([Brick help center](https://brick.frontkb.com/en/articles/6572993)). One snippet suggests Strict Mode can also disable emergency unbricks, which conflicts with Brick's own help text; test this before relying on either behavior. One Brick can serve several people, each with their own account ([Brick FAQ](https://getbrick.com/pages/faq)).

On Android (12 and later), Brick uses an Accessibility Service to watch app launches, and Strict Mode adds Device Admin to block uninstalling ([Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.brickllc.brick&hl=en_US); [Brick Android support](https://brick.frontkb.com/en/categories/1717121-android-support)). Play Store reviews report that "users can bypass all bricked apps by opening them in a mini window" ([Habit Doom](https://habitdoom.com/blog/brick-alternative-android), a competitor). The same source counts about 3,960 Play reviews against more than 50,000 iPhone ratings, which makes Brick an iPhone-first product. On iOS its rating is **4.94 from 32,600 reviews** ([Bitrise](https://bitrise.io/resources/tools/app-navigator/apps/ios/com.brickllc.brick-app)). Press coverage is the deepest in the category, and reviewers agree it works because you "can't cheat." NBC Select's two-week test found it "doesn't give the option to cheat out of it" ([NBC Select](https://www.nbcnews.com/select/shopping/brick-phone-app-blocker-review-rcna259740)). Consumer Reports saw some days under three hours of screen time but called it "one of the better stopgap solutions" that "didn't solve the problem outright" ([Consumer Reports](https://www.consumerreports.org/electronics-computers/cell-phones/can-brick-solve-my-screen-time-problem-a3152096219)). Forbes Vetted tested it for over a month and tried to hack it in both regular and Strict modes ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/forbes-personal-shopper/article/brick-review/)). Reviewers also flag crashes during setup, the ease of forgetting to allow-list banking or rideshare (editing requires unbricking first), hyperlink leaks and an unpopular navigation redesign ([Cybernews](https://cybernews.com/reviews/brick-phone-blocker-review/); [App Store reviews](https://apps.apple.com/gb/app/brick-ditch-distractions/id6448794069?see-all=reviews)). The strictness also cuts the other way: if you leave home without the puck, "you cannot unBrick remotely" ([NBC Select](https://www.nbcnews.com/select/shopping/brick-phone-app-blocker-review-rcna259740)).

### Bloom is a $39 steel card built around breaks and families

Bloom is a stainless-steel NFC keycard from Find Your Bloom LLC, sold at bloom.inc (not the unrelated "Bloom" investing, therapy or finance apps). It costs **$39 with a free app, "no subscriptions or in-app purchases,"** and a 30-day money-back guarantee ([bloom.inc](https://bloom.inc/products/bloom-card)). There is also a 20% student discount ([Student Beans](https://www.studentbeans.com/student-discount/us/bloom-inc)), and it is sold on Amazon ([Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/Bloom-Card-Blocker-Subscription-Free-Stainless/dp/B0GWYMDX4F)). Two college students founded it in 2024. They took **$75K for 20% from Daniel Lubetzky on Shark Tank** (Season 17), after reporting $220K in six months of sales ([SharkTankBlog](https://www.sharktankblog.com/business/bloom/)). Fortune reported more than 60,000 units sold by February 2026 ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/02/13/analog-gen-z-phone-addiction-bloom-brick-app-blockers-dumb-phones-social-media/)). One competitor blog claims Bloom carries a "$47 device plus subscription" ([Detach](https://getdetach.app/blog/bloom-subscription-cost-over-time)). That contradicts Bloom's own listing and every other source, so disregard it.

The tap mechanics match Brick's, but the philosophy doesn't. Bloom lets you add **one to three five-minute breaks per session** that unlock apps without the card and relock on their own ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/02/13/analog-gen-z-phone-addiction-bloom-brick-app-blockers-dumb-phones-social-media/)). Schedules start and end automatically ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/bloom-conquer-screen-time/id6736728158)). You get **three emergency exits, plus one more every month** ([Bloom FAQ](https://bloom.inc/pages/faq)), so a determined user always has a card-free way out each month. **Child Mode** "disables emergency exits, prevents app deletion, and locks schedules… Only a parent's card can change the settings" ([Bloom FAQ](https://bloom.inc/pages/faq)); neither Brick nor any other product found offers an equivalent. Strict Mode prevents deleting apps during a session ([bloom.inc](https://bloom.inc/)). A 2026 update also stopped users force-closing the app, a gap early Android reviewers exploited "in three quick touches" from the recent-apps screen ([Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.findyourbloom&hl=en_CA)). Sources conflict on which Strict Mode protections reached Android versus iOS, so verify on your own device. On Android, Bloom uses the Accessibility Service API ([Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.findyourbloom&hl=en_US)). Pixel users called the lockout "far too easy to bypass" (the developer says this is fixed), and a Samsung S26 Ultra user needed a reinstall before it worked ([Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.findyourbloom&hl=en_US)). iOS ratings are **4.8 from about 751 ratings** and Android **4.39 from about 960** ([AppBrain](https://www.appbrain.com/app/bloom-conquer-screen-time/com.findyourbloom)). Complaints cover schedules that fail to start, crashes in Apple's app picker, and a few card or account failures that ended in refunds ([App Store reviews](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/bloom-conquer-screen-time/id6736728158?see-all=reviews)). No major tech outlet has published a hands-on review. Coverage is Fortune's trend piece, Shark Tank recaps, affiliate blogs and YouTube, where one 30-day review is titled "This app gave me anxiety" ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KzyO3yj1XHY)). The researchers could not confirm whether a generic NFC sticker can stand in for the card.

Two independent-looking comparisons agree on the core trade-off: "Unlike Brick, which is a total block, Bloom offers breaks," and Brick feels "smoother and more seamless" ([autonomous.ai](https://www.autonomous.ai/ourblog/brick-vs-bloom); [Wholistic Productivity](https://wholisticproductivity.com/brick-vs-bloom-which-physical-app-blocker-is-worth-your-money/)). Autonomous sells a competing $9 key, and the second site is likely an affiliate, so treat both with caution. The verdict: **if you already know you'd use every break Bloom offers, buy Brick.** Brick also has a quieter advantage over Bloom. The magnetic puck naturally lives on a fridge or in a car, while a card slides into the wallet you carry everywhere, which defeats the "key somewhere else" model unless you store it away deliberately. Bloom wins on price, on Child Mode, and on its 30-day refund guarantee, which makes it the lower-risk experiment.

## A dozen other keys and boxes fill every price point

### Seven tokens beyond Brick and Bloom, from $9 to a subscription

The token market has grown well beyond the two headline brands. The most interesting newcomer is the **Autonomous Key**. It costs **$9 with no subscription**, runs on iOS 15+ and Android 8+, and one key pairs with multiple phones. It began shipping in August 2026 after a Kickstarter campaign ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/01/this-9-key-physically-locks-your-most-addictive-apps/)). Its design is stricter than Brick's. Apps are **locked by default**, each tap opens a 60-minute window, and then apps relock automatically ([autonomous.ai](https://www.autonomous.ai/ourblog/brick-vs-bloom), the vendor's own blog). With a lock-by-default design, there is no session to force-close and no forgetting to start one. The catch is that it has almost no user track record.

**ScreenZen Halo** ($49) is a different idea. It is a Bluetooth pebble that blocks chosen apps whenever the phone is within a set radius of up to about 50 feet, for example the bedroom. The ScreenZen app is free, one Halo covers unlimited devices, and its replaceable batteries last "a couple of years" ([ScreenZen](https://screenzen.co/products/halo)). Apartment Therapy found it "worked well most of the time," with occasional delayed activation that later updates reduced ([Apartment Therapy](https://www.apartmenttherapy.com/halo-screenzen-review-37554918)). It is the only product built for **location rules** ("no scrolling in bed") rather than time or tap rules.

The subscription tokens cost more over time. **Unpluq** sells its tag for $26.50, but "you can't use the tag without a subscription." Premium prices are reported at "from $29" ([Unpluq store](https://www.unpluq.com/collections/all)), about $64 a year ([whatifididnt](https://whatifididnt.com/blog/unpluq-review/), an affiliate) and an older $79 including the first year ([Vice](https://www.vice.com/en/article/unpluq-tag-dumb-capabilities-smartphone/)). In return you get start-and-end schedules and software unlock challenges (shake, scroll, steps, QR) that work even without the tag. **Blok** (iPhone) sells NFC cards, keychains and magnets from $29, plus the app at **$59.99 a year or $9.99 a month**. It markets anti-deletion, emergency unblocks, accountability, and the ability to use any barcode as a key ([Blok](https://www.blok.so/resources/blok-vs-opal-screen-time-app); [App Store](https://apps.apple.com/ie/app/blok/id6477306136)). Blok also writes many of the "best blocker" roundups, so its self-rankings are marketing.

The rest are niche. **BLOCC** is an iPhone NFC tag at €39.99 one-time, with lifetime premium for three devices and gamified "tower building" ([BLOCC](https://www.getblocc.com/)). **Norma** is an iPhone-only 70 mm steel disc with no subscription; its price didn't surface, and an optional stand costs €20 ([Norma](https://nor.ma/)). Unbranded Amazon clones such as the **Fruaros** blocker copy the Brick format ([Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/Fruaros-Physical-Distractions-Routines-Moonstone/dp/B0H69TJPST)). **focusLock** (iOS) is the only product found that can use an **Apple Watch** as the key, alongside any NFC tag ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/focuslock-your-key-to-focus/id6504141777)). **Opal sells no hardware**; it remains a software subscription. The researchers searched for "Tapuz," "Ground," "Unbrick," "Lock-in," "Stay Focused Key," "Focus Dock" and "Hold" as blocker hardware and found nothing, so treat those names as non-existent or unverified. No product uses a smart ring or a **Clicks keyboard** as the key. The Power Keyboard has no blocking function in any source. Sticking an NFC tag on it would be self-defeating anyway, since the keyboard lives on the phone; that point is the researchers' inference.

### Timed lockboxes are the only lock software can't touch

Physical containment is the one category no settings menu can undo. The **kSafe** (Kitchen Safe) locks for anywhere from one minute to ten days, and "remains locked until the timer reaches zero. No overrides!" ([kSafe](https://www.thekitchensafe.com/)). Mini and Medium sizes cost $59 and the XL $69 ([Mashed](https://www.mashed.com/1071329/heres-what-happened-to-kitchen-safe-after-shark-tank/)). The Mini fits phones only up to about 5.8 inches, so a current Pro Max needs the Medium. The **Mindsight Timed Lock Box** starts at $39.95, runs from one minute to 30 days, and has three modes. Its Fortress Mode has no unlock code; the only emergency override is emailing support ([Mindsight](https://mindsightnow.com/products/timed-lock-box); [Mindsight FAQ](https://mindsightnow.com/pages/faq)). **Yondr** makes the locking pouches used by schools and venues. A pouch needs a magnetic unlocking base, which makes it close to useless for solo self-control ([Yondr](https://www.overyondr.com/phone-locking-pouch)). Its consumer **Home Tray** costs about $215–$249 and is described as signal-blocking with charging pass-through ([Yondr](https://www.overyondr.com/home-tray); [Pittco](https://www.pittcomanagement.com/products/phone-lock-box-yondr-home-tray-lockable-signal-blocking-phone-lock-box/13478304/)). One review says it doesn't block signal ([whatifididnt](https://whatifididnt.com/blog/phone-lock-box/)), so check the version. **Aro** is a charging box for up to four phones plus a membership of about $9.99 a month, and "there is no physical lock" ([Aro](https://www.goaro.com/membership); [Mindsight](https://mindsightnow.com/blogs/mindful-matters/aro-box-alternative-the-best-screen-time-solution-for-real-digital-balance), a competitor). That makes Aro an honor system with streaks, not a lock.

The lockbox's strength is also its flaw for this brief. A locked phone can't pay, navigate, show a 2FA code or call a ride. **A lockbox is therefore the right tool for the phone you don't carry**: the home entertainment phone in a two-device setup, or your only phone during evenings and nights at home.

## Free methods match paid tokens on iPhone and nearly do on Android

### Foqos is Brick without the price tag

On iPhone the standout free option is **Foqos**. It is free, open source, and has no account, ads or in-app purchases. It runs on the same Screen Time API as Brick, and blocking profiles can be triggered by an NFC tag, a QR code or barcode, a timer, Shortcuts or a schedule ([foqos.app](https://www.foqos.app/); [GitHub](https://github.com/awaseem/foqos)). It works with standard **NTAG213** stickers and publishes a 3D-printable "NFC brick" and keychain design. The project is active: about 842 GitHub stars, with updates on the day of this research. It holds a **4.9 rating from about 814 App Store ratings** ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/foqos-tap-to-block/id6736793117?see-all=reviews&platform=iphone)). Coverage bills it as "a $9 alternative to the Brick" (Foqos plus a 20-pack of tags from Amazon) ([Dorm Therapy](https://www.dormtherapy.com/foqos-app-diy-brick-phone-lock-100012155)). Users praise it as "absolutely premium without a price" and report occasional crashes and flaky calendar automation. The research gap is anti-delete. No source confirmed whether Foqos has a Brick-style Strict Mode, so assume deleting Foqos ends a session. Close that gap with iOS's own "Deleting Apps: Don't Allow" restriction behind a Screen Time passcode (covered below). A sibling project, **Family Foqos**, adds three emergency unblocks per configurable 2–8-week reset period ([family-foqos.app](https://family-foqos.app/)). **Jomo** also supports NFC tags, but only NTAG215 ([Jomo help](https://help.jomo.so/en/article/how-to-unblock-block-apps-with-a-nfc-tag-ng6zur/)).

Apple's Shortcuts app can run an automation when you tap an NFC tag, for example switching a Focus mode ([Apple](https://support.apple.com/en-tj/guide/shortcuts/apde31e9638b/ios)). DIY write-ups such as a homemade "Focus Brick" and the "Bricked" Shortcut use exactly this ([Jacob Desforges](https://jacobdesforges.com/nfc-focus-brick/); [heliomass](https://heliomass.com/posts/108-bricked---a-shortcut-for-reducing-distractions/)). The researchers found no Shortcuts action that actually blocks an app. Focus modes filter notifications and home screens, which is why a Foqos-type app is still needed for a real shield.

### Android has three free open-source NFC blockers

On Android the free equivalents are **TapBlok**, **nfcGuard** and **Lock – NFC App Blocker**. TapBlok is open source with no in-app purchases or tracking, and uses an NFC tag or QR code to start and end sessions. It can also be driven from Tasker, MacroDroid or Samsung Routines, and was updated in September 2026 ([tapblok.com](https://tapblok.com/); [GitHub](https://github.com/cajdata/TapBlok/)). nfcGuard is on Google Play with schedules ([Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.andebugulin.nfcguard)). Lock is free with no analytics, account or internet access. It pairs up to five tags, offers grace periods and emergency unlocks, and **requires Android 14+** ([Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.nathanb.lock&hl=en)). Early Android ports of Foqos also exist, one with a Settings-blocking strict mode and a three-tap "break glass" exit ([GitHub](https://github.com/Elias02345/Foqos-Android)). All of these share Android's Accessibility ceiling. They are fine as a speed bump, but none holds up against someone who knows about safe mode. Samsung owners should know that Routines can be triggered by an NFC tag, but "Routines can't restrict app usage like modes can" and "can't turn on a mode" ([Samsung Community](https://us.community.samsung.com/t5/Samsung-Apps-and-Services/Please-add-the-ability-for-a-Routine-to-activate-a-mode/td-p/2686965)). So a tag can't trigger Samsung's built-in app restrictions directly.

### The Screen Time passcode trick is the strongest free lock on iPhone

Apple's built-in Screen Time becomes a real lock once three things are in place. First, set a Screen Time passcode. Second, turn on "Block at Downtime" or "Block at End of Limit," so that extra time requires the passcode ([Apple](https://support.apple.com/en-lk/guide/iphone/set-up-screen-time-for-yourself-iphbfa595995/14.0)). Third, close the known loopholes. Keep "Set Time Zone Automatically" on with Location Services set to "Don't Allow Changes," which stops the clock trick. Set "Deleting Apps" to Don't Allow, and use "Block at End of Limit" to kill endless "One More Minute" taps ([Protect Young Eyes](https://www.protectyoungeyes.com/blog-articles/12-ingenious-screen-time-hacks-how-to-beat-them)). The weak point is the reset path through your Apple Account. Three commitment tricks close it: having a partner set the passcode, setting it through a **burner Apple Account** whose password you lock away ([WaitToUnlock](https://waittounlock.com/blog/lock-yourself-out-of-screen-time)), or using a time-delayed passcode vault ([Password Locker](https://password-locker.com/)). Both of those last two are vendor services. Combined with the iOS 26.4 option that protects blocker permissions with the same passcode, this setup makes **any** iOS blocker (Brick, Bloom or Foqos) far harder to escape. That is why the researchers regard it as more important than which token you buy. For a mild extra drag, set grayscale as the triple-click Accessibility Shortcut ([Apple Support](https://support.apple.com/en-sa/111773)).

### Android's built-in tools are designed as nudges, not locks

Android's built-in tools are gentler by design. Digital Wellbeing's Focus mode lets you tap a paused app for a **five-minute override** ([Android Police](https://www.androidpolice.com/2020/08/06/digital-wellbeing-lets-you-override-focus-mode-for-5-minutes-apk-mirror/)). App timers are "a nudge, not a hard block" ([How-To Geek](https://www.howtogeek.com/443322/how-to-set-app-time-limits-and-block-apps-on-android/)). Pixel's Bedtime mode and Samsung's Modes (including Sleep mode, which can go grayscale and restrict apps) add schedules ([Zapier](https://zapier.com/blog/bedtime-mode-android/); [MakeUseOf](https://www.makeuseof.com/bedtime-mode-sleep-mode-android-samsung/)). Family Link gives an outsider real control, but Google built it for children and teens. One review says that as a self-control tool it "has very limited protection" ([Tech Lockdown](https://techlockdown.com/blog/family-link-review)), and whether Google allows it on an adult account wasn't confirmed.

## Software-only blockers rent you strictness by the year

A software-only blocker earns its place through its strongest "can't quit" mode, and nearly all of those sit behind a subscription. The strongest iOS options are **Opal Deep Focus** (sessions can't end early, deleting Opal doesn't lift the block, and websites are covered), **Jomo Strict/Hard mode** (rules locked for a chosen number of days) and **Refocus Strict Mode**. On Android, **AppBlock Strict Mode** can disable uninstalling until time is up, and **Lock Me Out premium** adds anti-uninstall and anti-tamper protection plus a "lock screen only" mode. The friction apps, **one sec** and **ScreenZen**, deliberately don't hard-block. They interrupt the impulse and let you continue.

Launchers are the lightest layer. On Android, **Olauncher** (free, open source) shows only the time and a few app names ([Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=app.olauncher&hl=en_US)). Minimalist launchers can cut use substantially for some people; one How-To Geek writer said a launcher "helped me cut my phone use in half" ([How-To Geek](https://www.howtogeek.com/this-android-launcher-helped-me-cut-my-phone-use-in-half/)). The Communicator's Message Hub is built with Niagara Launcher, which also runs on the Titan 2 ([Android Police](https://www.androidpolice.com/niagara-launcher-unihertz-titan-2/)). On iPhone, **Blank Spaces** costs $17.99 a year or $23.99 lifetime ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/blank-spaces-launcher/id1570856853)), **Dumbify** costs $4.99 once ([dumbifyapp.com](https://dumbifyapp.com/)), and the **Dumb Phone** app is free ([WhistleOut](https://www.whistleout.com/CellPhones/Apps/dumbphone-homescreen-app-review)). iOS launchers only restyle the home screen, since Search and the App Library still reach every app. They add friction to a blocker; they don't replace one.

Compared on cost, a one-time token is the clear winner for anyone past about a year. Brick costs less than seven months of Opal, and Bloom about five. Among the subscriptions, only Freedom offers cross-device blocking that a token can't match. That matters if your relapse pattern is to switch to the laptop.

## Evidence backs the category of intervention, never the brand

No peer-reviewed study has evaluated Brick, Bloom, Unpluq, Opal, ScreenZen, Jomo, AppBlock or any lockbox, and no study compares a hardware token with a software blocker. What exists is evidence for *kinds* of intervention. The best evidence comes from these studies:

**Self-set limits.** In Allcott, Gentzkow and Song's field experiment (n = 1,933), simply giving people a screen-time limit tool cut social media use by **24 minutes a day (17%)**. Paying them $2.50 an hour cut it by 39%, and the authors attribute **31% of social media use to self-control problems** ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28936)). That 31% is the market commitment devices serve.

**Friction prompts.** In the one sec field study, target-app openings fell **57%** after six weeks, and users abandoned 36% of attempts at the prompt ([PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2213114120)). One co-author is one sec's developer.

**Blocking mobile internet.** The strongest causal result is Castelo et al.'s preregistered trial (n = 467). Two weeks of blocking all mobile internet with Freedom, while calls and texts still worked, improved well-being, mental health and sustained attention, and 91% of participants improved on at least one measure ([PNAS Nexus](https://academic.oup.com/pnasnexus/article/4/2/pgaf017/8016017)). Among participants who kept the block on for at least 10 of the 14 days, daily screen time fell from **314 to 161 minutes** ([USRTK](https://usrtk.org/healthwire/blocking-mobile-phone-internet-may-boost-mood-mental-health-attention/)). That qualifier says many participants didn't keep it on.

**Grayscale.** One week of grayscale cut daily use by 37.9 minutes ([Holte & Ferraro](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/03623319.2020.1737461)), and a review of grayscale studies reports 22–50 minutes ([Dekker & Baumgartner](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/20501579231212062)).

**The broader literature is more modest.** A 2024 meta-analysis of 18 detox studies found small well-being gains (SMD 0.21–0.27) ([CBSN](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1089/cyber.2023.0742)). A 2025 trial of *planning* a detox found no significant effect on total usage ([Computers in Human Behavior](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0747563225000718)).

Read together, the studies show a gradient. **The larger the share of the internet a tool removes, and the harder it is to undo, the bigger the effect, and the more people quietly drop out.** Brick-style tokens sit in a useful middle position. They block whole categories, like the Castelo intervention, but they keep the essentials running, which should make them easier to stick with. That last point is an inference, since no one has measured adherence to tokens. The main evidence on tokens themselves is reviewer testimony: Consumer Reports' "stopgap" verdict ([Consumer Reports](https://www.consumerreports.org/electronics-computers/cell-phones/can-brick-solve-my-screen-time-problem-a3152096219)), App Store ratings from a self-selected, hardware-buying audience, and vendor sales figures. The failure modes are well documented: disabling your own blocker in cycles ([drgore](https://drgore.substack.com/p/blockit)), getting used to friction prompts, platform bugs, and billing traps on subscriptions.

## Match the lock to your phone and your budget

The right lock depends on three things: which phone you carry, how often you'd give in to a break, and whether you'll pay. The recommendations below keep the device advice from the earlier research. The **Unihertz Titan 2 Elite ($489.99)** is shipping now with NFC and Google Pay ([TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/computing/unihertz-titan-2-elite-business-phone-review)). The **Clicks Communicator** costs **$499 until September 30, then $649**, and ships in December 2026 ([GlobeNewswire](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/09/18/3364666/0/en/Clicks-Communicator-Gets-Upgraded-Specs-and-Expanded-Ecosystem.html?f=22#038;fvtc=5&)). Both have NFC, run Google Play and run Android 12 or later (the Titan on Android 16, the Communicator on 17). So Brick, Bloom, Lock, TapBlok and the Play Store blockers should all install on them. Nobody has yet published a hands-on test of any blocker on either phone.

A few specifics make these setups work. On an iPhone with Brick, **build the allow-list before your first session**, because editing it means unbricking. Include Phone, Messages, Wallet, Maps or Google Maps, every bank and card app, Uber and Lyft, your authenticator and password manager, work MFA, Calendar and Camera. Then block every browser, since in-app browsers and non-Safari site rules leak. That list is the researchers' inference; Brick publishes no official "essentials" template. Put the puck where getting it takes effort: the fridge, the glove box, or with a partner ([Pocket-lint](https://www.pocket-lint.com/brick-device-phone-app-blocker/)).

On an Android keyboard phone, the carried device should simply never have the feed apps installed, as recommended in the earlier device research. The blocker's job then shrinks to guarding the browser and perhaps YouTube or email, which is why a free Lock or TapBlok setup is enough there. Spend any hardware budget on the phone left at home, not on the one you carry. Two cautions apply to the Communicator. First, turning on Android 17's Advanced Protection will silently disable Accessibility-based blockers ([Android Police](https://www.androidpolice.com/advanced-protection-mode-android-17-beta-accessibility/)). Second, its hardware airplane-mode switch is a free, zero-software way to go offline ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clicks_Communicator)). Your Clicks Power Keyboard plays no part in blocking. It stays useful on whichever slab phone you keep, and it lets you run the whole plan now as a two-week trial: current phone, Power Keyboard, Foqos or Brick. That trial is the cheapest way to learn whether a lock alone is enough before paying $489.99–$649 for a separate phone. It can't end before the Communicator's $499 price expires on September 30, so that reservation has to be decided on conviction, not on trial results.

If you're on the fence about Brick versus Bloom, apply one test. Think back to the last time an app offered you "15 more minutes." If you took it, buy Brick. If you ignored it, Bloom's breaks are a comfort rather than a trap, and you save $20.

## Snippet-only research leaves specific gaps

The research team's direct page fetches were blocked by the network proxy for getbrick.com, bloom.inc, the App Store, whatifididnt.com, TechCrunch and others. Every fact above therefore comes from search-engine snippets of the cited pages. Some snippets merged text from several pages, so a few attributions are the most plausible source rather than a certain one. Much of the comparison literature comes from companies that sell a competing blocker: Habit Doom, Blok, BLOCC, Apptoken, Detach, Autonomous, Mindsight, unhookd, Password Locker, WaitToUnlock, Lockpact and Unstar. Other sources, notably whatifididnt.com (which offers discount codes), Stories of a Millennial and Wholistic Productivity, appear to be affiliate blogs. Independent sources with real weight are fewer: NBC Select, Consumer Reports, Forbes Vetted, Fortune, TechCrunch, Apartment Therapy, Android Authority, Android Police, Apple and Google documentation, and the peer-reviewed studies. Reddit sentiment from r/nosurf, r/digitalminimalism and r/dumbphones could not be retrieved.

Several specifics remain unresolved. For Brick, it is unclear whether the five emergency unbricks are per device or per account, whether Strict Mode disables them, and whether schedules now support end times. For Bloom, it is unclear which Strict Mode protections have reached Android and whether a generic NFC tag can replace the card. No source said whether Foqos has any anti-delete protection. Unpluq's renewal price conflicts ($29, $35 or $64 a year), and the Norma, Lock Me Out, Stay Focused and StayFree prices never surfaced. The iOS 26.4 Screen Time permission protection is documented only by Tech Lockdown and developer forums, and a Face ID bug report puts its reliability in question. Finally, no blocker has been tested on the Titan 2 Elite, and the Communicator has no production units yet. Treat all prices as snippet-verified, not checkout-verified, and confirm them before buying.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question from "which blocker is strongest" to "where does the escape hatch live." On iPhone, Brick, Bloom, Foqos and Opal all rely on the same Apple framework. The biggest step up in strictness is therefore free: the iOS 26.4 permission protection behind a passcode you can't reset. The token you buy mostly decides how the lock *feels*: a fridge puck, a wallet card or a $9 key. On Android, no app-level lock holds against a determined owner, so the phone itself has to be the restriction. That is the real case for a keyboard phone carrying only essentials, and it matters more than the keyboard.

The most useful combination turns out to be one no single product sells. Carry a phone whose feed apps simply aren't installed, and put the strongest lock, even a no-override lockbox, on the entertainment phone at home, where locking everything costs you no essentials. Because tokens and timers are cheap and the Titan 2 Elite ships today, a missed Communicator deadline costs money, not the strategy. The studies suggest the outcome will depend on how much you remove and whether you keep it removed, not on which brand you pick.
